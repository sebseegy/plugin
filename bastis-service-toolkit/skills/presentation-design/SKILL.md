# Presentation Design Skill

Your job is not to make slides. It is to make slides that *communicate*.

The foundation is David JP Phillips' five cognitive design principles from his TEDxStockholmSalon talk, combined with Echo Rivera and Garr Reynolds' visual storytelling approach.

The core insight: **your brain cannot read and listen simultaneously.** The moment text appears on screen, the audience reads it and stops hearing you. Everything that follows is about working *with* the brain instead of against it.

---

## Phase 1: Discovery

Before touching any slides, establish four things. Extract from context or ask directly:

1. **Core message** — What is the single most important thing the audience must leave knowing? One sentence.
2. **Audience** — Who are they and what do they already know? (Executive, technical, client, internal team?)
3. **Context** — Meeting type, duration, how many slides is appropriate, projected in a room or shared as a document?
4. **The ask** — What should the audience DO or DECIDE after seeing this?

A deck built without knowing the ask is always wrong, regardless of how polished the slides look.

---

## Phase 2: Narrative Spine (structure before slides)

Build the story first. Slides are chapters of a story, not containers of information.

```
1. HOOK         — Start with tension, a striking fact, or the cost of the problem.
                  Never start with an agenda slide.
2. CONTEXT      — The situation. What's true right now that makes this matter?
3. JOURNEY      — 3–5 acts that build the argument or tell the story.
4. RESOLUTION   — Clear recommendation or call to action.
5. PROOF        — Evidence, methodology, backup detail.
                  Lives in the appendix — never in the main deck.
```

**Every slide earns its place** by answering "so what?" in one sentence. If you can't articulate why a slide exists, cut it or merge it.

## Phase 3: The 5 Principles (David JP Phillips)

These are cognitive and neurological rules, not aesthetic preferences. Apply them to every slide.

### Principle 1 — One message per slide

One idea. One takeaway. One assertion.  
If a slide has two messages, it needs to be two slides.

The brain's cocktail party effect applies to slides: when two things compete for attention, the audience picks one and misses the other. Make the choice for them by never presenting the choice.

**The slide headline must be the takeaway, not the topic label.**  
❌ "Q3 Revenue" → ✅ "Revenue grew 40% QoQ, ahead of plan"  
❌ "Next Steps" → ✅ "Three actions before Friday"

### Principle 2 — The redundancy effect (no text + speech simultaneously)

This is the most violated rule in corporate presentations.

John Sweller and Mayer's research: if you have full sentences on your slides and speak at the same time, retention approaches **zero**. The audience's working memory (already weak — like trying to remember seat 42 on a train ticket) cannot handle two streams of language at once.

**The fix:** Pull all your detailed text into the speaker notes field. The slide surface is for images, short fragments, and key numbers — not sentences.

Your slides are visual cues. Your voice carries the message. The slides are not the presentation. **You are the presentation.**

### Principle 3 — Size = importance

Every time you open your eyes, your brain automatically focuses on: moving objects, signalling colours, high-contrast objects, and **large objects**. This is biological, not optional.

The most important element on a slide must be the largest element on that slide.

The problem: every default PowerPoint template makes the headline (a topic label) the biggest element. The audience spends 70% of their attention on the least important thing. Reduce the headline. Make the key data, key quote, or key number the dominant visual object.

If your audience's eyes go somewhere you didn't intend, it's because you made something else the biggest. Fix the size, not the audience.

### Principle 4 — Contrast controls focus

Contrast tells the eye where to go. Use it deliberately.

When everything on a slide has equal weight, the audience's eyes scatter. When one element has dramatically more contrast (lighter on dark, bolder, more saturated), the eye goes there first, second, and third.

**Dark backgrounds are the right default for presentations projected in a room.**  
White backgrounds make the screen the most contrast-rich, largest object in the room — competing with the presenter. A dark background lets the presenter become the biggest, most contrast-rich element. The slide becomes a backdrop, not a competitor.

Use contrast within slides to guide the sequence of attention:  
- Show the full slide with low-contrast supporting elements
- Highlight the key number or line in full contrast
- Grey everything else out while discussing the key point
- Bring back the rest when moving on

### Principle 5 — Maximum 6 objects per slide (target: 3)

The perceptive limit: when you show 1–3 objects, people *see* them instantly (0.2 seconds). When you show 5–7 objects, people must *count* them — which takes 500% more cognitive energy.

Once the brain has to count rather than see, it tends to give up. That is death by PowerPoint.

Count every element: text block, image, chart, icon, divider line, logo, page number, label. **If you have 7 or more objects, the audience will spend 500% more energy just parsing the slide — energy they do not spend understanding your message.**

More slides with fewer objects is always better than fewer slides with more objects. There is no limit on slide count. There is a limit on objects per slide.

**Never add page numbers.** They either cause anxiety ("83 more of these?") or false hope ("almost done"). Remove them from every template.

## Phase 4: Color & Visual Language

**Palette:**
- 1 primary accent color (used sparingly — one key element per slide)
- 1 dark background (dark navy, charcoal, or near-black — not white for projected presentations)
- 1 light text color (white or light grey)
- Maximum 3 colors total. 2 is better.

**Exception:** for decks that will be printed or shared as PDFs rather than projected, a light background is acceptable. Ask if unsure.

**For client work:** use the client's brand colors if known. If not, use a palette that fits their industry vertical. Match the energy of their brand — don't impose your defaults.

---

## Phase 5: Font Discipline

- **Sans-serif only**: Arial, Calibri, Inter, Helvetica, Outfit
- **Body text minimum**: 24pt — ideally 28–32pt
- **Headlines**: 36–60pt — don't be conservative here
- **Maximum 2 fonts** per deck
- If someone in the back row can't read it without squinting, it is wrong. This is the designer's fault, not the audience's.

**No full sentences in bullets.** Bullets are prompts for the speaker, not the script. The full sentence belongs in the presenter's mouth, not on the slide. Use: bold fragments, data points, or short claims.

❌ "Our solution enables teams to collaborate more effectively across time zones and departments"  
✅ "Async-first. No timezone tax."

---

## Phase 6: Data Visualization

Every chart answers exactly ONE question. That question is the slide headline.

**Format rules:**
- Remove: gridlines, borders, chart backgrounds, decorative elements, legends placed far from data
- Grey out background/secondary data; make the insight pop in the accent color
- Labels go directly on or next to the data point
- Axis labels: large, readable, include units

**Chart type:**
- Comparisons → bar charts
- Trends over time → line charts
- Part-to-whole → horizontal bar; avoid pie charts
- Never use 3D charts, dual-axis unless unavoidable

If a chart takes more than 5 seconds to interpret, it must be simplified before it goes on a slide.

## Phase 7: Output

### Step 1 — Plan the deck
Produce the full slide plan using this format:

```
SLIDE [N]: [Headline as takeaway — not a topic label]
Visual: [What goes on this slide — image, chart type, 1-3 fragments, key number]
Objects: [Count of objects on this slide — must be ≤ 6]
Speaker note: [What the presenter says — 2–4 sentences of spoken narrative]
```

### Step 2 — Review the plan
Before building, run this checklist on every slide:
- [ ] One main idea only
- [ ] Headline is a takeaway, not a topic label
- [ ] Object count ≤ 6 (target: ≤ 3)
- [ ] Largest element = most important element
- [ ] No full sentences in bullets
- [ ] All key content is in speaker notes, not on the slide
- [ ] "So what?" answerable in one sentence

### Step 3 — Build the file
After the plan is confirmed:
- **For .pptx files**: invoke the `pptx` skill with the slide plan as the content spec
- **For monday.com client presentations**: invoke the `monday-presentation-design` skill
- **For HTML/web decks**: build a self-contained HTML presentation

## Never Do These

- More than **40 words** on a slide (aim for under 15)
- White backgrounds for projected presentations
- Page numbers (remove from every template)
- Clip art, stock handshakes, lightbulbs, jigsaw puzzle metaphors
- WordArt, drop shadows on text, gradient text fills
- Distracting animations (spinning, bouncing, flying in)
- Starting with "Today I will be talking about..."
- Fonts below 24pt on any projected slide
- Full sentences in bullet points
- Speaking while displaying a text-heavy slide

---

## The One-Line Test

> "Your audience can either read your slides or listen to you speak. They cannot do both." — Echo Rivera

If someone could understand your full presentation just by reading the slides without hearing you speak, the slides have too much text. The presenter is the presentation. The slides are the backdrop.
